Threat actor use of AI becoming routine, warns Google
<p>Threat actor use of AI in malicious cyber campaigns is evolving at pace, with several trends emerging over the past three months that point to a fast-approaching inflection point, according to research published this week by the <a href="https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/agentic-threat-activity-crosses-a-significant-threshold" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Google Threat Intelligence Group</a> (GTIG) and Mandiant.</p> <p>Google’s team said that while previously it had only observed “nascent adversary interest” in agentic capabilities, since the summer multiple threat actors have started to abuse open source, agentic pen-testing frameworks to automate elements of their intrusions.</p> <p>“Threat actor adoption of these frameworks represents a paradigm shift towards intrusions occurring at machine speed, with the automated chaining of complex attack paths scaling operations significantly,” the team wrote in its report.</p> <p>“While the mechanics of the intrusions remain the same, such as using compromised credentials and targeting unpatched systems, the speed and scale at which frameworks can exploit these known weaknesses introduces a new defensive challenge.”</p> <p>Some of the more popular open source frameworks being turned to malicious ends include Artex – recently the subject of <a href="https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2026/261113.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">an FBI advisory</a> – Cairn, CyberStrikeAI and PentAGI, said Google. It said it had seen Cairn used in several attacks against targets in the aerospace sector in the US, while PentAGI has been spotted being used for recon and vulnerability scanning in cyber attacks against Asian organisations over the past couple of months.</p> <p>Google said that both financially motivated cyber criminal gangs and nation state-backed threat actors were likely using these frameworks, and said that their further use was a virtual certainty. Moreover, said the team, “As the sophistication of these tools and underlying LLMs increases, we anticipate not only reduced human involvement in these operations but also increased stealth and sophistication of the activity.”</p> <p>At the same time, threat actors are also using multi-agent systems and LLM-driven pipeline to automate the more labour-intensive parts of vulnerability analysis, including tearing down patches and weaponising proof-of-concept (PoC) exploits. This is a grave concern because, in doing so, they can cut the window between supplier patch disclosure and in-the-wild exploitation.</p> <p>The Google team suspects threat actors have realised it is more efficient to use LLMs to weaponise patched n-day flaws instead of uncovering new zero-days because they can zoom in on a finite set of source materials, compare differing source code versions, and examine PoC code, release notes, security research and even media coverage.
