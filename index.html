AI hasn’t changed ‘core skills’ needed for work, say experts
<p>Artificial intelligence (AI) has had a significant impact on both education and the world of work, but it has not changed the fundamental skills needed to enter the workplace, according to experts.</p> <p>During an education committee meeting focused on the use of AI and EdTech in education, several people from the field claimed that while <a href="https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366650228/AWS-AI-symposium-TeenTech-alumni-call-for-AI-literacy-in-schools">AI literacy will be important for young people</a> going forward, the core skills children should be learning to prepare them for their working futures haven’t changed.</p> <p>Rose Luckin, professor emerita at University College London, claimed the core of education is giving children the <a href="https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366537313/Tech-jobs-are-on-the-rise-but-soft-skills-are-most-coveted-says-WEF">soft skills they need for their lives</a>, and ensuring they continue to be “good” at learning – AI hasn’t changed this.</p> <p>“It’s perfectly clear the nature of work is changing and we have to help young people be prepared for that, but as previously, that’s not just about helping them use the tech,” she said.</p> <p>“We can’t predict exactly what their jobs will look like, but what we can predict is that they’re going to be really good at learning.”</p> <p>Oral communication, confidence, resilience, creative thinking, organisation, planning and the ability to work in a team were all offered as examples of the skills employers are looking for in young job seekers, with experts agreeing transferable skills are more important and other technical skills can be taught on the job.</p> <p>Josh Hillman, director of education at Nuffield Foundation, referred to them as “employability skills” – “transferable skills that are developed partially through the education system but then partially through those skills in work”.</p> <section class="section main-article-chapter" data-menu-title="Evolution required"> <h2 class="section-title"><i class="icon" data-icon="1"></i>Evolution required</h2> <p>However, <a href="https://www.computerweekly.com/blog/When-IT-Meets-Politics/How-do-we-ease-the-pain-of-transition-from-a-skills-system-that-fails-employees-and-employers">employers are increasingly expecting more</a>, and Hillman suggested the education system needs to “evolve to some extent” to combine transferable soft skills with a “foundational knowledge and judgement in AI”.</p> <p>“[Education] can’t possibly predict what the future needs,” he said.</p> <p>While core skills are the most important for ensuring young people are employable in the future, more could be done to give people the technology skills they’ll need for work – something that has <a href="https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252529226/Tech-skills-shortage-still-a-problem-for-employers">not just been an issue in the wake of AI</a>.</p> <p>As Patrick Milnes, head of policy for people and work at the British Chambers of Commerce, explained: “The skills system isn’t aligned with the pace of change that we’re seeing at the moment, so there is an urgent need to develop a serious plan for long-term AI literacy – and not just in education, but in lifelong reform of how people can adapt as the technology changes.”</p> <p>But on the other side of the coin, Kester Brewin, associate director at the Institute for the Future of Work, argued that “schools are being asked to do so much”.</p> <p>Schools are currently focused on taking the knowledge of teachers, passing it on to students, and assessing “how much of that transfer has happened” through exams and grades, said Brewin, who stated: “AI and an ongoing debate</a>.</p> <p>Schools exist to educate children, but cannot provide every skill needed for every job, and the curriculum is slow-moving, whereas while firms are responsible for training new and existing employees, they do expect some level of prior knowledge, and what skills are needed by workplaces is often not communicated to education providers.</p> <p>Milnes said: “Education providers and employers think about skills, not just AI skills but all skills, in quite a different capacity, and that can make joining up the two groups quite difficult in terms of preparing young people for the world of work.</p> <p>“We’d like to see more engagement with employers around all of this,” he added.</p> <p>Hillman also said it would be useful for young people to have more guidance surrounding careers so they can be better prepared.</p> <p>“Young people need to have accurate labour market information about what jobs are changing, what tasks are changing within
